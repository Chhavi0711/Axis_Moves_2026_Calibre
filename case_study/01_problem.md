# 01 - The Problem

## The brief we were solving against

Axis MOVES 2026 asked teams to act as Axis Bank's Transformation Council and design
**one** enterprise-level transformational change for 2035 - not a basket of small
improvements, and not a purely technical fix. The brief explicitly ruled out AI-chatbot
ideas, incremental process automation, product feature tweaks, and technology-only
solutions that don't change the operating model itself. Whatever we proposed had to
show a real change in roles, processes, and governance, with a quantifiable business
case behind it.

## Where we chose to look

The brief listed several illustrative areas teams could redesign - customer acquisition,
retention, segmentation, relationship management, wealth management, credit
underwriting, risk management, employee experience, people and careers, operations,
product innovation, and data/AI ecosystems. We deliberately did not treat this as a menu
to pick one item from. Instead, we looked for a *structural* bottleneck that sits
underneath several of these areas at once - because a fix at that layer would compound
across the bank rather than improve one function in isolation.

## The bottleneck we found: authority is fixed by title, not by judgment

Across lending, credit, and risk decisions, we identified a recurring structural issue:
**decision-making authority at Axis Bank today is allocated by designation and tenure,
not by demonstrated judgment quality.** A capable officer can reach the correct call on
a case and still have to route it through several layers of sign-off, purely because
their title sits below the threshold the process requires - regardless of how reliably
that officer's judgment has performed in the past.

## The anchor case: Anjali

We grounded the problem in a concrete scenario rather than an abstract statement:

A merchant, Anjali, receives her largest export order to date and urgently needs working
capital. Her Relationship Manager is confident the loan should be approved. But because
authority is tied to designation, the request has to move through multiple approval
layers regardless of the RM's judgment. Twelve days later, the decision comes back -
matching exactly what the RM recommended at the start.

The problem was never the quality of the RM's call. It was how that judgment had to
travel through the organization before it could be acted on.

*A note on this story: the submitted deck keeps Anjali's case deliberately spare -
"her biggest export order," no further detail. In the team's own prep material and in
how we narrated the pitch live, we gave her more texture - a garment exporter based in
Tirupur - to make the case concrete for a jury hearing it spoken rather than read. That
detail is scene-setting we added in delivery, not part of the two-slide submission
itself; we've kept the two clearly separate here rather than implying the deck said
more than it did.*

## Why this is a strategic problem, not an operational annoyance

We framed this specifically as a **governance and decision-making** problem, not a
staffing or workload problem, because:

- The organization already had the right answer early - the delay was structural, not
  analytical
- Fixing it with more headcount or faster manual approvals would still be bound by the
  same title-based routing logic
- By 2035, every bank will have comparable AI tooling. What differentiates one bank from
  another at that point is whether the *organization itself* is built to recognize and
  act on good judgment quickly - which is an operating-model question, not a technology
  question

This framing is also why we treated AI as an enabler of routing, not as the source of
the decision itself - directly in line with the brief's instruction to focus on
organizational transformation rather than a technology-only fix.
